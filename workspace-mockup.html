5.3 — The Citric Acid Cycle

Before the citric acid cycle can begin, pyruvate produced during glycolysis must be converted to acetyl CoA, a two-carbon compound, by the pyruvate oxidation step. This occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.

In the citric acid cycle, the acetyl group of acetyl CoA joins with oxaloacetate (a four-carbon compound) to form citrate (a six-carbon compound). The cycle then regenerates oxaloacetate at the end of each turn, oxidizing organic fuel and releasing CO₂.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ~36–38 ATP

Per turn of the cycle, the following electron carriers are produced that feed the electron transport chain:

  • 3 NADH — carry high-energy electrons
  • 1 FADH₂ — another electron carrier
  • 1 ATP (or GTP) — direct energy output
  • 2 CO₂ — released as waste

Because each glucose molecule produces two acetyl CoA molecules, the cycle runs twice per glucose, yielding 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, and 2 ATP from this stage alone.

5.4 — Oxidative Phosphorylation

The majority of ATP synthesis occurs in oxidative phosphorylation via the electron transport chain (ETC) and chemiosmosis. NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

As electrons move along the chain, energy is released and used to pump H⁺ ions (protons) from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient.

ATP synthase (Complex V) uses the flow of protons back into the matrix (driven by the electrochemical gradient) to power ATP synthesis — this process is called chemiosmosis.

~32–34 ATP produced via ETC + chemiosmosis per glucose

Oxygen serves as the final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and protons to form water. Without oxygen, the chain would stop and ATP production would halt.

Can you explain the difference between aerobic and anaerobic respiration? I'm having trouble with when cells use each one.

Great question! The key difference comes down to oxygen availability and ATP yield:

Aerobic respiration requires oxygen and is far more efficient — producing approximately 36–38 ATP per glucose molecule. It runs through three stages: glycolysis → pyruvate oxidation → the citric acid cycle → electron transport chain (ETC) + chemiosmosis.

Anaerobic respiration occurs without oxygen and only generates 2 ATP per glucose via glycolysis alone. Cells use this pathway when oxygen is limited, such as during intense exercise (lactic acid fermentation in muscles) or in yeast (alcoholic fermentation).

Think of aerobic as a slow-burning, highly efficient furnace, and anaerobic as a quick-burst, low-yield generator.

Walk me through the citric acid cycle step by step.

The citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and runs twice per glucose. Here's each step:

  1. Step 1: Acetyl-CoA (2C) + Oxaloacetate (4C) → Citrate (6C)
  2. Step 2–3: Citrate → Isocitrate (rearrangement)
  3. Step 4: Isocitrate → α-Ketoglutarate + CO₂ + NADH
  4. Step 5: α-Ketoglutarate → Succinyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH
  5. Step 6: Succinyl-CoA → Succinate + ATP/GTP
  6. Step 7: Succinate → Fumarate + FADH₂
  7. Step 8: Fumarate → Malate (water added)
  8. Step 9: Malate → Oxaloacetate + NADH (cycle complete)

Net per turn: 3 NADH · 1 FADH₂ · 1 ATP · 2 CO₂. Oxaloacetate is regenerated, ready for the next turn.
